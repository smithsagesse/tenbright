  Summary
  Phase 3 defines how GMIA looks, sounds and flows: a proposed child-facing brand
  ("Tenbright"), an original mascot built around the ten-makes-one idea, the Number
  Kingdom world, child and parent journeys, a screen-by-screen interface spec, and a
  usability test plan for Gate G3. The visual system and the key screens are built as
  separate design artifacts so they can be edited and reused in the build.

  Approvals carried forward (Phase 2, approved Nov 3)

         Review budget of about $5–9k and the lean, founder-led build path.
         Piano Quest engine sharing stays open; the learner and parent models are generic so
         the choice can wait.
         Mascot naming moved to this phase (section 2).

  Companion artifacts (built Nov 3)

         Tenbright Design System: 10 colors in light and dark themes, type, spacing, radius,
         brand rules and a cover. Components are added once the prototype settles.
         Tenbright Brand and Screens: logo and palette, Pip's character sheet, the game cover,
         the Kingdom map (C2), a working practice item (C5) and the parent home (P3). The
         map's Pip button opens the practice item in Play mode.


  Brand strategy and naming
  Recommendation: keep "Global Mathematics Intelligence Academy" as the company and
  institutional brand, and give children and families a short, warm product name. The
  working pick is Tenbright: it names the core idea (ten ones make a ten), suggests a bright
  mind, and is easy for a 7-year-old to say.

  Mascot and characters
  The mascot is Pip, a small glowmoth who collects light-beads. Ten beads click into a
  lantern, and ten lanterns stack into a tower: the character's world literally performs place
  value, so every animation teaches regrouping.

  Why this concept

         It turns the MVP's core idea (10 ones = 1 ten, 10 tens = 1 hundred) into the mascot's
         everyday job, so the art reinforces the math instead of decorating it.
         Beads connect visually to the soroban, and lanterns connect to base-ten blocks; one
         visual language covers three representations.
         It is original: not an animal from a famous franchise, not a number-shaped character,
         no borrowed style.

  Supporting cast (Number Kingdom)

         Marta the Mapkeeper: an older tortoise who guards the Kingdom map and explains
         new regions. Calm, appears at unlocks.
         The Rounders: three hill goats who argue about which ten a number is closer to; they
         front the rounding skills.
         Captain Carry and Lieutenant Lend: a pair of river-ferry operators who move beads
         across columns; they front regrouping in addition and subtraction.

  All characters avoid stereotyped accents or cultural shorthand; human characters in story
  scenes rotate names, skin tones and family structures.

  Child and parent journeys · first week

  Child journey, step by step

     1. Meet Pip (about 2 minutes): Pip's lantern goes dark; the child helps relight it by
        building a number. This teaches the interface by doing.
     2. Explore the map (placement, 15 minutes or less): presented as visiting regions; the
         map lights up where the child is already strong.
     3. Daily session: Pip suggests one activity chosen by the engine; the child can pick
         between two options to keep a sense of control. Each session ends at a natural break
         with a "see you tomorrow" screen, never a cliffhanger.
     4. Certificate moments: after the 30-day check, the child sees a lantern ceremony; the
         certificate itself is sent to the parent side.

  Parent journey, step by step

     1. Sign up: email and password, then verifiable consent before any child data is
         collected.
     2. Add a child: nickname and birth year, plus "What math is your child working on now?"
     3. Hand over the device: a child-mode lock (a 4-digit parent PIN) keeps the child out of
         settings and billing.
     4. Placement summary: "Already strong at, ready to learn next, we'll strengthen first."
     5. Weekly report (email plus dashboard): what the child understands, what they're
         working on, the likely reason for any difficulty, one 10-minute home activity, and how
         progress will be measured.

  Lesson screen layout (C5, the most-used screen): question and narration at top; the
  manipulative in the center (60% of the screen); the answer pad on the right for right-
  handed default, mirrored by a setting; Pip small in the corner, animating only when spoken
  to.


  Accessibility and child-safety design rules
  GMIA designs above the legal minimum because 7-year-olds' motor control and reading
  are still developing: WCAG 2.2 AA requires 24 × 24 CSS-pixel targets (Deque), and Apple
  and Google recommend 44 and 48, so child screens use 64 × 64 px minimum and 88 px for
  primary actions.

  Accessibility

          Targets: child 64 px minimum, 88 px primary, 16 px gaps; parent 44 px minimum.
          Contrast: text 4.5:1 minimum and 7:1 for numbers in items; never rely on color alone
          (correct and incorrect also use shape and sound).
          Audio-first: every instruction narrated, with captions; replay always available;
          adjustable speech rate.
          Reading level: child text at or below grade 1 for ages 7, grade 2 for 8–10.
          Motion: reduced-motion setting honored; no flashing above 3 per second.
          Dragging has a tap-to-place alternative; no time limits on standard items; a dyslexia-
          friendly font option.
          Screen-reader labels on parent pages; child manipulatives expose their current value
          to assistive tech.

  Child-safety design

         No links out of the child side; no ads; no chat; no other users visible.
         Parent PIN guards settings, billing and any outbound link.
         No push notifications to the child's device; reminders go to the parent, opt-in.
         Session caps enforced from the parent setting; a gentle end screen, never a hard cut
         mid-item.
         No collection of the child's real name, photo, voice or location in the MVP.


  Prototype and Gate G3 test plan
  G3 passes when 5–8 children aged 7–9 and their parents use a clickable prototype of the
  first-session flow and at least 4 of 5 success criteria are met; target date is February 2027.

  Prototype scope: C1 → C2 → C3 → C4 → C5 → C6 → C7 for one micro-skill (AS05, adding 2-
  digit numbers by composing a ten), plus P3 and P4 with sample data. Built as an
  interactive web page with working blocks and number pad, not static images, so children
  can actually try the manipulative.

  Sessions: 30 minutes per child with a parent present, one moderator, one note-taker;
  consent and an age-appropriate assent script; children are thanked with a small non-cash
  gift (e.g., a book).

  Recruiting: homeschool co-ops and family networks in Gwinnett County; mix of ages 7, 8
  and 9; at least two children who find math hard; at least one child who uses assistive
  features.

  Decisions for approval and sources
  Five decisions close Phase 3's design direction; once approved, the next step is the G3
  prototype build and usability sessions, then Phase 4 engineering.

  Decisions

         Name: Tenbright as the child-facing product name, subject to a trademark search
         (Class 9 and 41) before final logo files.
         Mascot: Pip the glowmoth, with the lantern and tower system for place value.
         Palette and type: the night-lantern palette with Nunito and Atkinson Hyperlegible.
         Prototype scope for G3: map → AS05 practice item → feedback → session end, plus
         the parent home; the working practice item on the canvas is the starting point.
         Commission budget for final art and sound (estimate $8–25k, from the Phase 1
         budget), or continue with these drafts through the pilot.

  What the practice-item prototype already does: shows 38 + 27 with beads and lanterns;
  Make a ten trades 10 ones for a lantern; the number pad and Check work; answers 515, 55
  and 75 trigger the matching clue from the misconception library; the Hint button walks the
  first three hint levels.

  What is not built yet: audio narration and Pip voice, animation, the other 11 screens, real
  data, and accessibility testing. These are deliberately left for the G3 build.
