  Executive summary
  Recommendation: build one narrow, provably effective product first — a mastery-
  based number-sense and arithmetic app for ages 5–8, with a parent co-play dashboard
  and one money simulation — and treat the lifelong ecosystem as the long-term
  roadmap, not the first release. The vision is sound; the scope as written is a 10-year, multi-
  million-dollar program, not a launchable product.

  Five corrections to the brief, each explained in its section:

     1. Stage 1 (12–24 months) should not be an app. The AAP tells app makers to stop
        building apps for children under 18 months until benefit is shown. Deliver it as a
        printable caregiver guide and physical kit.
     2. Simulated AI experts are a design tool, not a review. Child safety, COPPA and early-
        math validation need a small paid panel of real humans before launch.
     3. Vedic mathematics has weak evidence; mental abacus has mixed evidence. Both
        belong as optional enrichment tracks from about age 7–8, not as the core method.
     4. An open-chat AI tutor for children is the highest-risk feature. A 2025 PNAS field trial
        found unguarded GPT-4 access lowered later test scores. The MVP tutor is a
        constrained hint engine, not free chat.
     5. The market is crowded with strong free products. Khan Academy Kids is free;
        Prodigy's math content is free. Differentiation must be transparent mastery and
        parent partnership, not more games.

  Decision asked of the founder: approve the ages 5–8 MVP scope, a year-one cash budget
  of roughly $44k–$132k (estimate, section 11), and recruiting three paid human advisors
  before any child data is collected.

  Decision rights
         Product lead (founder): final call on scope, budget, priorities.
         Veto holders: privacy counsel (any data collection), early-childhood advisor (anything
         shown to under-8s), math educator (any item marked mastery-bearing). A veto must
         cite a written reason; disagreements are logged, not hidden.
         Simulated reviewers: draft, critique and red-team. They can block a release only by
         raising an issue a human then confirms.


  Review workflow per feature
     1. Feature brief (the 10 fields in section 7) drafted.
     2. Simulated cross-functional critique: math, child-development, safety, engineering,
         business. Each writes risks and a pass/fail.
     3. Founder resolves conflicts; dissent recorded in a decision log.
     4. Build behind a feature flag.
     5. Human advisor review for anything child-facing or data-touching.
     6. Test with 3–5 children in a supervised session; record task success and confusion
        points.
     7. Ship, then measure against the feature's stated metric at 30 days.

  Cost: three advisors at roughly 2–4 hours a month each, estimated $3k–$12k a year in
  total; privacy counsel review estimated separately in section 11. These are estimates, not
  quotes.

  2. Product vision and strategic positioning
  Position GMIA as the math program parents can verify: it shows exactly what a child
  has mastered, retained and can apply, and it makes the parent a partner rather than a
  bystander. Incumbents compete on game engagement and free content; few compete on
  transparent, durable mastery plus money skills.


  Market signal
         The need is real. On NAEP 2024, U.S. 4th-grade math was still 3 points below 2019 and
         8th grade was 8 points below; gains since 2022 came only from students at or above
         the median (NCES/NAGB release, Fox/CITC summary).
         The niche is modest in size. One analyst firm sizes the global kids' math-app market
         at about $727M in 2026, growing about 8% a year (360iResearch via
         MarketResearch.com). Treat paid analyst figures as directional only; estimates vary
         widely.
         Subscriptions are hard here. RevenueCat's 2026 report lists Education among the
         toughest categories for new apps to reach revenue thresholds (9to5Mac). On the plus
         side, Education has the lowest first-month share of annual cancellations, about 30%
         (9to5Mac).

  Positioning statement
  For parents of 5–8-year-olds who want proof, not screen time, GMIA is a math program
  that builds number sense to durable mastery through short play sessions, physical
  activities and real-world money missions. Unlike engagement-first math games, it reports
  what a child has truly mastered and retained — and never upsells to the child.


  Defensible advantages (in order of durability)
     1. A validated mastery record (immediate + 7/30/90-day retention + transfer) that
         parents and later schools trust.
     2. Parent co-play system: offline activities, printable missions, physical manipulatives
         tied to the digital record.
     3. Money and entrepreneurship strand from age 5, which most early-math apps lack.
     4. Child-first trust posture as a selling point: no ads, no third-party trackers, no child-
         facing purchases.

  Who it serves first: U.S. families, English, ages 5–8, starting with Georgia (your home
  market and a standards set you can align to). Schools are a year-2+ channel after
  evidence exists.

  3. Educational philosophy and evidence standards
  Every method in GMIA carries one of four evidence tiers, and only Tier 1–2 methods may
  drive mastery decisions. The core pedagogy rests on practices with strong evidence:
  developmental progressions, concrete-to-abstract representation, retrieval, spacing and
  interleaving.

  Principles
     1. Understanding before speed. Timed modes unlock only after untimed accuracy is
         mastered; speed is reported separately.
     2. Developmental progression, not grade level. The IES guide's first recommendation is
         to teach number and operations along a developmental progression (IES). Age sets
         safety and content bounds; demonstrated mastery sets progression.




                                                                                                   Page 6 of 27
GMIA Phase 1 Executive Package




     3. Mix practice. In a 787-student, 54-class RCT, interleaved practice beat blocked
        practice on a delayed test, 61% vs 38% (Rohrer et al. 2019). GMIA review sets mix
          earlier skills by default.
     4. Multiple representations, no learning-style labels. Every concept is shown visually,
        physically and symbolically to every child.
     5. Caregiver as co-teacher. For under-6s, the app prompts shared play and offline
        activity.
     6. Honest claims. No marketing claim about outcomes until GMIA's own pilot data
        supports it.


  Screen-time boundaries (binding product rules)

  benefit is shown (AAP summary via Fairplay; Allegro Pediatrics summary).

  MVP skill sequence (Stage 4, first release ~30 nodes)
     1. Diagnostic entry: subitizing, counting on, number bonds to 10.
     2. Number bonds to 10 → doubles and near doubles → make-ten addition.
     3. Subtraction as missing addend → think-addition strategy → subtraction within 20.
     4. Place value: tens and ones → numbers to 100 → compare and order.
     5. Add and subtract within 100 using tens, then mixed strategies.
     6. Money: coin values, counting mixed coins to $1, making change from $1.
     7. Word problems: join, separate, compare types.
     8. Time to the half hour; equal sharing as the bridge to division.

  5. Mathematical mastery framework
  A skill is "Mastered" only after independent accuracy across varied items on two
  separate days plus one transfer item; it becomes "Durable" only after passing a 30-day
  retention check. Completing a game, level or world never grants mastery by itself.

  Learning cycle per skill
  Diagnostic → concept introduction → visual explanation → guided demonstration → guided
  practice → independent practice → real-world application → immediate assessment →
  delayed retention → mastery record. These are the brief's ten steps; GMIA enforces them
  in order for mastery-bearing skills.


  Retention scheduling
  Default checks at 7, 30 and 90 days, then adaptively: a skill answered quickly and
  correctly gets a longer next interval; a slow or wrong answer shortens it. Review items are
  interleaved with current work rather than presented as a separate "review chapter,"
  consistent with the interleaving evidence in section 3.

        as a kit), virtual abacus mirrors it, mental visualization only after bead fluency. Entry
        from age 7, with a spatial-working-memory-friendly path (slower fading of the visual
        abacus) for children who struggle.
     2. Measure abacus competence separately — bead-reading accuracy, physical-abacus
        accuracy, mental accuracy, speed — and never let abacus results substitute for core
        arithmetic mastery.
     3. Vedic techniques are taught as "why it works" algebra lessons. Example: multiplying
        numbers near 100 (97 × 99) is shown alongside the standard algorithm and the

          Learner: nickname, avatar, age band, accessibility settings. No real name, birthdate,
          photo, voice or location.
          SkillNode / Item: content IDs and versions (from the content bundle).
          Attempt event: learner, item, answer, correct, response ms, hint level, timestamp,
          session.
          MasteryState: learner × skill → state, evidence counts, next review date.
          Mission run: sim inputs and outcomes.
          Audit log: every admin read of learner data.


  AI tutor design (MVP)
     1. Deterministic first. Each item ships with three authored hints and misconception-
        specific feedback; the tutor picks among them from the child's error pattern.


                                                                                                         Page 18 of 27
GMIA Phase 1 Executive Package




     2. LLM used offline, not live, for children. The LLM drafts items, hints and explanations
        at authoring time; a human reviews before they ship. This avoids live hallucinations
        reaching a 6-year-old.
     3. Live LLM only in the parent area ("explain this skill and give me a kitchen-table
        activity"), with guardrails and no child data beyond skill IDs.
     4. Guardrails grounded in evidence: in a ~1,000-student field experiment, unrestricted
        GPT-4 practice access raised practice scores but cut later exam performance by
        about 17%; a tutor with hint-only safeguards largely removed the harm (Bastani et al.,
        PNAS 2025; Wharton summary). GMIA's tutor never gives the final answer before two
        hint levels and a retry.


  Engineering standards from day one
  Git with protected main branch · CI running type checks, unit tests on all arithmetic
  generators and content validation · automated accessibility checks · error reporting
  without child identifiers · nightly encrypted backups with a tested restore · feature flags ·
  semantic-versioned content bundles · written release checklist.

  Mascot concept (original)
  "Pip," a small round seed-sprite who grows one leaf for every Durable skill. Pip doesn't
  know math; the child teaches Pip, which makes explaining (a mastery signal) part of the
  story. Pip never shows sadness at mistakes — Pip says "let's try a new way." Supporting
  cast: a tortoise shopkeeper (money), a fox engineer (geometry), a heron abacus-keeper
  (Phase 6). All characters are original and must be drawn by a contracted illustrator with
  full IP assignment.


  World design for the MVP
  Number Kingdom is a garden-village that grows as skills become Durable: bridges built by
  place value, a market opened by coin skills, a lemonade stall run by the child. Growth is
  tied to retained learning, not time played, so the visible reward and the educational record
  are the same thing. Later worlds from the brief (Multiplication City, Fraction Forest,
  Financial Freedom Island and so on) attach as new regions of the same map.


  Design principles
     1. Calm, not stimulating: muted, warm palette; no flashing, no slot-machine reveals, no
        loot boxes, no streak-loss shaming.
     2. Readable without reading: every instruction is spoken; icons over text for ages 5–6.
     3. Physical-digital bridge: every world has printable missions and a household-object
         version.
     4. Accessibility built in: WCAG 2.2 AA target, color-blind-safe coins and counters,
        reduced-motion mode, switch-friendly targets.
     5. Sound identity: soft marimba and woodblock palette; each strand has a three-note
        motif; a quiet "skill grew" chime replaces fanfare.

  12. Risks, dependencies and mitigation
  The three risks that can end the project are a children's-privacy violation, scope creep
  beyond a part-time team, and a product that engages but does not measurably teach.
  Each has a named control and an owner.


  Child trust and compliance baseline (binding)
         COPPA: the FTC's amended COPPA Rule has been fully enforceable since April 22,
         2026. It adds separate parental consent for disclosing children's data to third parties,
         written security programs and data-retention policies (Hunton, Finnegan via
         Mondaq). Civil penalties reach about $51,744 per violation (summary; confirm with
         counsel).
         Apple Kids Category: apps should not include third-party analytics or advertising and
         may not send personal or device information to third parties (Apple guideline 1.3
         summary). GMIA's first-party-only design satisfies this by construction.
         GMIA rules: no ads ever; no child-facing purchases; parental gate on every link,
         setting and payment; no child voice, photo, real name, birthdate or precise location
         collected in the MVP; learner data deletable by the parent in one action; retention
